# User Research

## Goal
I wanted to understand how university students keep track of their academic and personal responsibilities. I wanted to know where things slip, why they slip and what students have already tried.

## Method
* **Survey:** Google Forms, 22 responses, September 2026
* **Interviews:** 3 one on one interviews, about 15 minutes each

## Key findings

### 1. Memory is the main system
17 of 22 survey takers rely mostly on memory. 8 use memory and nothing else. All 3 interviewees keep most tasks in their heads.

### 2. Overload is the real cause, not missing info
"Too many things at once" was the top reason for forgetting (11 people). Only 3 said they missed the announcement. Info is spread out (12 of 22 get it from 3 or more places) but a good class rep often fixes that by hand. P2 had no real class rep and still said scattered info was "not the main issue."

### 3. Graded work gets protected. Everything else slips.
Students guard what affects their marks. What they forget is outside that. P1 forgot a club recruitment interview. P2 regularly forgets the classes she teaches. P2 put it simply: assignments don't slip "because those are marked."

### 4. Plans break when something new shows up
P2 set up a weekly plan, then a new quiz was announced and she had to fix everything by hand. P3 said his day falls apart when someone hands him a task he didn't plan for.

### 5. Planning tools fail because of setup effort
P2 tried a notes app and Google Calendar. Both failed. Setting up the calendar took too long, her week kept changing and her planning day kept getting taken over by other things.

### 6. Overload costs sleep before deadlines
18 of 22 say school and personal tasks compete at least sometimes. P1 and P2 both said sleep and health take the first hit. P3, with a light load, said grades would go first.

### 7. No deadline means no action
P1 and P2 both struggle with tasks that have no due date. P2 has a project she started long ago and still hasn't finished.

### 8. Students blame themselves
When asked what they'd change, 8 of 15 survey answers were about their own habits, like "be more disciplined" or "make a routine." Only 1 asked for a tool feature. P1 said she'd stop waiting for the deadline. P2 said she'd be more disciplined.

## In their words
> "It happened more times than I would like to admit." (P2, on forgetting classes she teaches)

> "Setting up the Google Calendar is really a problem." (P2)

> "It takes a toll on my physical health, on my sleep schedule." (P1)

> "If it's important enough, I'll remember it." (P3)

## Who this is for (and who it is not)
The pain shows up most for students with a heavy load outside class. All 3 survey takers who forgot something more than 5 times handle 2 or more outside responsibilities. P3, with a light load, had no real problem. CampusFlow should focus on busy students, not every student.

## Problem statement
Busy students who juggle classes with tuition, clubs, work or family keep most of their tasks in their heads. They protect graded work, but classes they teach, club commitments and personal tasks slip. When a new task shows up, their plan breaks. Planning tools take too long to set up and keep updated, so students give up on them and blame their own discipline.

**How might we** help busy students keep a changing week in view without the setup work that makes them quit?

## Limits
* Small sample: 22 survey responses and 3 interviews
* Most people come from one or two universities in Bangladesh
* A few survey rows are low quality (mostly "Other" answers, one contradiction, one off topic answer). I kept them in the counts but did not quote them.
* Only one light load student was interviewed. More heavy load interviews would make the findings stronger.

## What I'd do with more time
* Interview 2 more heavy load students, including one with a paid job
* Grow the survey to 50 responses across more universities
* Test the problem statement with a quick concept sketch before building personas
